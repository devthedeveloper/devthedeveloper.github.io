10buyers a week

A spreadsheet and three hours of DMs a day

The client had built a real following. What he did not have was a shop. Stock lived in a spreadsheet. Orders arrived as Instagram messages. Payment was a screenshot of a bank transfer. He spent about three hours every day typing "still available?" replies.

The first version I built was deliberately small: product pages, a cart, a checkout, and one admin screen where he could add stock. Two weeks of work. No queue, no caching, no clever engineering — because at ten buyers a week, none of that would have earned its keep.

This matters for the rest of the story. Every piece of complexity below was added because something specific broke, and not one day earlier.

3,000people, one evening

The drop that broke it

He announced a limited release. Forty pairs, 6pm on a Friday. Around three thousand people showed up at once.

The site was unreachable within a minute. That was bad. What was much worse: it sold fifty-one pairs of a forty-pair release. Eleven customers paid for shoes that did not exist. He spent the weekend refunding people and apologising publicly.

Two problems, not one. A slow site is an inconvenience — people are annoyed and come back. Selling stock you do not have is a broken promise, and it costs you the customer permanently.

So I fixed the overselling first, even though the outage was the thing everybody was shouting about. That order of operations was the single most valuable decision in this project.

Here is why it happened, in plain terms. When three thousand people click "buy" in the same second, the shop checks stock three thousand times at once. Every one of those checks looks at the count before anyone else's purchase has been written down. All three thousand see "40 available" and all three thousand are told yes. The shop was answering honestly — it just could not see the future.

The five fixes, in the order they were needed

Each one was shipped on its own, measured on a real drop, and only then followed by the next.

800buyers  ·  fix one

Put the box behind the counter

Adding a pair to your cart now takes it off the shelf and holds it in your name for ten minutes, exactly like a shop assistant putting the box behind the counter while you find your card. If you pay, it is yours. If you do not, it goes back on the shelf automatically and the next person can have it.

Stock is counted in one place, once, and nowhere else. Two people can no longer be told yes for the same pair — the second person is told the truth immediately instead of being charged and disappointed later.

Result: zero oversold pairs since. That number has held for eighteen months.

5,000buyers  ·  fix two

Build a line outside the door

A shop with one door cannot let five thousand people in at once. Nobody expects it to. So we stopped pretending and built the queue that already existed in real life.

When a drop opens, everyone gets a numbered place in line. The store admits as many people as it can genuinely serve, and the rest wait on a page that shows their position and roughly how long it will be. Your place is held even if your phone drops signal.

Customers stopped seeing error pages. That was the visible win. The quieter win is that an honest wait makes people calmer than a fast site that fails — they can see the queue moving, so they trust it.

12,000buyers  ·  fix three

Separate the browsers from the buyers

On drop day, roughly nine out of ten visitors are looking, not buying. They are refreshing the release page, checking their size, showing a friend.

Looking is cheap. Buying is expensive. So the pages people look at are served from a pre-made copy that costs almost nothing and loads instantly, while the checkout talks to the live system. The result is that the expensive part of the shop only handles people who are actually spending money.

Same hosting bill, roughly ten times the crowd.

30,000buyers  ·  fix four

Get the bots out

By this point almost half of every drop was being bought within four seconds by automated resale software. Real customers were furious, and rightly — they were queueing for a release that was already gone.

Four changes, none of them dramatic on their own:

  • Accounts must be verified before they can enter a drop, and verification takes long enough to be inconvenient to do a thousand times.
  • One pair per person per release, checked against payment and delivery details, not just the email address.
  • The queue opens on a signal that cannot be predicted from the outside, so software cannot be pointed at the exact millisecond.
  • Suspicious orders are held for a human to look at rather than cancelled automatically — because getting this wrong and cancelling a real customer is worse than letting one bot through.

Bot share of stock fell from roughly 48% to 6%. The client's community noticed within one drop, and said so loudly.

100,000buyers  ·  fix five

Never take money twice, never lose an order

At this size, rare problems stop being rare. One in a thousand is a hundred people.

Every payment now carries a single unique ticket. If someone double-taps the pay button, or their connection drops and the app retries, the ticket is already spent and the second attempt does nothing. Nobody gets charged twice.

And the shop no longer assumes a payment worked. It waits to be told by the payment provider, even if that confirmation arrives thirty seconds late. If a payment succeeds but the confirmation is lost in transit, the order still completes on its own. Orders and money can no longer disagree with each other.

How a drop works now

This is the whole customer journey on release day. Seven steps, and the client can explain every one of them to a customer without calling me.

  1. Announced

    Date and time posted. Sizes visible, stock hidden.

  2. In line

    Queue opens. Everyone gets a number and a wait estimate.

  3. Your turn

    Admitted to the store. Ten-minute window starts.

  4. Pair held

    Your size comes off the shelf, reserved in your name.

  5. Paid

    One payment ticket. No double charges, no lost orders.

  6. Confirmed

    Email and order page update the moment payment clears.

  7. Shipped

    Packing list generated. Tracking sent automatically.

Underneath, an order can only ever be in one of a few states, and every path out of every state is planned for — including the ones nobody likes to think about.

How I work

The same five habits produced every result above. They are not specific to sneakers.

  1. Watch first

    A week observing how you actually run the business before I write anything.

  2. Stop the bleeding

    The smallest change that ends the most expensive problem, shipped first.

  3. One thing at a time

    Each change goes out alone so we can tell what worked.

  4. Rehearse the big day

    Before every major release I simulate the crowd and find the weak point early.

  5. You watch it, not me

    A dashboard you can read yourself: people in line, pairs left, orders per minute.

That fourth habit is worth expanding on, because it is the one clients are usually surprised by. Two days before a big release I run a rehearsal: fake customers, real volume, everything switched on. Roughly one rehearsal in three finds something that would have gone wrong live. Fixing it on a Wednesday afternoon costs an hour. Fixing it at 6pm on release day costs the drop.

Nothing here is exotic. Most of the work was choosing what not to build, and doing the boring thing in the right order.

What it runs on

Ordinary, well-understood tools. That is on purpose — the client needs to be able to hire someone else to maintain this if I get hit by a bus.

  • Node.jsThe engine the shop runs on. One language across the whole system.
  • PostgreSQLThe ledger. Stock, orders and money live here, and only here.
  • RedisFast short-term memory. Runs the queue and the ten-minute holds.
  • Background workersEmails, refunds, releasing expired holds. Work that must not block a customer.
  • React and Next.jsWhat the customer sees. Product pages are pre-built so they load instantly.
  • StripePayments. They handle card data so we never touch it.
  • Load testingThe rehearsal. Simulates a hundred thousand people before the real ones arrive.

Hosting is standard cloud infrastructure, kept deliberately boring. No part of this design depends on a single provider's proprietary product.
