Five agents, one inbox, and a hard rule against guessing

Once the sneaker store could handle a hundred thousand people, it started collecting something else: twelve hundred customer messages a week. This is the system that answers most of them, and the rules that stop it inventing things.

Solving one problem uncovered the next

The store rebuild fixed drop day. Releases stopped falling over and post-drop complaints dropped from roughly two hundred to fifteen.

But the customer base kept growing, and everyday questions grew with it. Not the dramatic ones — the ordinary ones. Where is my order. Does this run small. Can I return these. When is the next release. By early last year that was around twelve hundred messages a week across Instagram, WhatsApp and email.

The client had two support staff. On weekends the first reply took about nine hours, because nobody was working. Sneaker customers are impatient and public about it. He was quoted a plan to hire three more people.

Hiring would have worked. It would also have cost roughly what the entire store rebuild cost, every year, forever. Worth checking whether the questions actually needed a person first.

What people actually ask

I read six weeks of messages and sorted every one by hand. Two thousand of them. It is dull work and it is the only reason the rest of this project went anywhere.

The top four categories are 84% of the inbox, and every single one of them can be answered from data the business already holds. Nobody was asking anything clever. They were asking questions that had exact answers sitting in a database, and waiting nine hours for a human to go and look.

That 84% is the ceiling, not the target. Some of those messages are angry, or confused, or three questions at once. I planned for well under the ceiling from the start.

My first attempt was worse than nothing

I built the obvious thing first: one AI assistant, all the company's rules in one long instruction, connected to everything. It took two days and it was genuinely bad.

  • It invented tracking numbers. Confidently. In the right format.
  • It quoted a fourteen-day return window when the policy is seven for sale items.
  • It could not reliably tell "where's my order" from "where's my refund", which are different questions with very different answers.
  • Every rule I added to fix one topic quietly broke another. The instruction reached four pages and became impossible to reason about.

One assistant that knows everything is one assistant that can confuse anything with anything. The fix was not a better prompt. It was giving each kind of question its own specialist, with access to its own data and nothing else.

Five agents, not one brain

The system works the way a good shop floor works. Someone at the front listens to what you need and walks you to the right person. That person knows one subject properly and can open their own filing cabinet — and only theirs.

Here is each one, and the exact jobs it can do. Nothing outside this list is possible — not discouraged, not unlikely, but impossible.

Front desk

Reads the message, works out what is really being asked, and hands it to one specialist. It answers nothing itself, which means it cannot get a fact wrong. If it cannot tell what the customer wants, it asks one short clarifying question.

Three rules that make it safe to trust

Most of the engineering here is not the agents. It is the fences around them.

No agent answers from memory

Every factual claim in every reply has to come from a lookup. If a tool returns nothing, the honest answer is that we don't know and a human is coming — not a plausible guess in the right format.

This is enforced in code, not requested in an instruction. A reply containing an order number, a date or an amount that did not appear in a tool result gets stopped before the customer sees it. That single check is what killed the invented tracking numbers.

Anything that costs money asks first

Looking things up is free and happens automatically. Starting a return, cancelling an order, or issuing a refund shows the customer exactly what is about to happen and waits for a yes. The agent can prepare an action. It cannot commit one on its own.

It hands off early rather than fighting

Certain things send a conversation straight to a person, immediately, no matter what it is about: anger, a mention of a chargeback or legal action, a third message from the same customer within a day, or any question about whether a pair is authentic.

That last one is deliberate. Authenticity is the most sensitive subject in this business, and an automated reply about it — even a correct one — reads as a brush-off. Some questions need a name attached.

A support system that hands off too often is mildly disappointing. One that confidently tells a customer something false is a screenshot on Instagram. Those risks are not equal, and the design is not balanced between them.

How it's tested

Three hundred real past conversations, each with the answer a good human support agent actually gave. Every change to any agent runs against all three hundred before it goes anywhere near a customer, and gets a score.

The first version scored 61%. It shipped at 92%, and the number is checked weekly.

The rule that keeps it honest: any answer that reaches a customer and turns out to be wrong gets added to the test set that same day. The suite only grows, and it grows fastest in exactly the places the system is weakest.

There is also a Monday morning review. The client and I read every conversation the agents handled where the customer replied unhappily. It takes twenty minutes. It has caught more real problems than any dashboard.

Two numbers there are worth being straight about. The satisfaction score is slightly below what the humans scored — people still prefer a person when they get one. It wins because a decent answer in eight seconds beats a better answer in nine hours, not because it is more likeable.

And roughly one conversation in four hundred still gets something wrong. That is not zero and it will never be zero. It is small enough that the Monday review catches every case, and every case makes the test suite stronger.

The real result is what the two support staff do now. They no longer type where-is-my-order forty times a day. They handle the hard 29% — the disputes, the authenticity questions, the customers who need someone to actually care. That is the part of the job worth paying a person for.

For engineers

The plain-English version is above. If you want the specifics:

  • Google ADKAgent Development Kit. The front desk is an LlmAgent with the five specialists passed as sub_agents, so routing is LLM-driven delegation rather than hand-written if-statements. Each specialist's description is what the router actually reasons over, so those descriptions get more editing than the instructions do.
  • GeminiModel behind each agent. The front desk runs a smaller, faster model than the specialists — routing is an easier job than answering.
  • ToolsPlain Python functions. Every one hits a read-only internal API in front of the store's Postgres, scoped per agent. No agent gets a database connection.
  • GuardrailsADK callbacks and plugins. before_tool_callback checks arguments and permissions before anything executes; after_model_callback is where the no-unsourced-facts check lives. Guardrails sit outside agent instructions on purpose — an instruction is a request, a callback is a rule.
  • Session stateCarries order context across a conversation so the customer isn't asked for their order number three times by three different specialists.
  • Evaluationadk eval against the 300-conversation evalset, wired into CI. A drop in score fails the build.
  • DeploymentCloud Run, alongside the existing Node.js store. The store and the agents share data, not a codebase.

The ADK dev UI earned its keep during the build. Being able to replay a conversation and see every routing decision, prompt and tool call in order is the difference between debugging an agent system and guessing at one.
